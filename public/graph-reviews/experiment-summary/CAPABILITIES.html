Grouping readings into capabilities

Per-commit reading produces one capability name per commit and no way to relate them. On the pilot that is 585 distinct names across 605 units, with only 15 phrases used more than once, covering 35 commits between them. The reading prompt explicitly asks for reusable wording — "use the same words you would use if you saw a related commit later, since these names are compared across commits" — and the words are in fact shared: course appears in 157 names, frontend in 91, etl in 75. It is the phrases that are unique, averaging 5.2 words. An independent per-commit call can be asked for a shared vocabulary but cannot be given one, because it has no access to the vocabulary.

npm run capabilities groups the readings four ways and compares them.

Correction: all four arms impose the wrong shape

The four-arm comparison is valid as a comparison of flat grouping methods, but not as a measurement of capability quality. After the measurements below were complete, the repository owner named roughly thirteen broad capabilities. The four arms return 81, 256, 287, and 398 groups; even an unambiguous 17-commit blog capability is split across 11–13 groups. All four methods are solving a task 10–50 times finer than the owner's concept of a capability.

The cause was partly designed in. The defaults were chosen to prevent any group from holding more than a tenth of the corpus, and groups over that size were called blobs. One owner-recognized capability covers roughly 28% of the pilot. The rule structurally forbids that answer. It is retracted: size can identify an umbrella to inspect, but cannot decide that it is invalid.

There is a second mismatch. These arms partition commits into one flat set, while one capability may contain meaningful sub-capabilities and a cross-cutting commit may support more than one. The measurements below remain useful for understanding evidence channels, model stability, and judging noise. Conclusions about which arm groups capabilities well do not survive the granularity error.

Blind hierarchical capability induction records the replacement experiment. It withholds the owner's list, infers umbrellas and children from the readings, and allows overlapping evidence.

Four arms, and why three of them share one routine

The first version of this comparison had two arms and could not interpret its own result. The baseline saw paths and subjects and clustered them; the model arm saw capability names and had a model group them. They disagreed at F1 0.090, and nothing in the design said whether the evidence or the method was responsible.

Three arms now share one clustering routine — the same mutual-kNN, the same neighbour count, the same floor — and differ only in which evidence channels the similarity may read.

combined is the plain mean of the other two, and a test holds it there. With no gold set there is nothing to tune a mixture against, so a hand-tuned one would only be fitted to whichever arm looked better afterwards.

The fourth arm, model, hands the capability names to x-ai/grok-4.6 and asks it to group them. It sees names alone — never paths, dates, authors, or another arm's clusters.

message is the commit subject plus its body, with merge provenance removed. Merge pull request #431 from AggieWorks/prefetch-api contributes prefetch-api and not AggieWorks: grouping commits by whose fork they arrived through is not a capability, and is contributor history being used to impose structure.

Clustering is by mutual nearest neighbours, not thresholded connected components. Single linkage collapsed the pilot into one cluster of 568 units out of 606 at every threshold tried. Requiring the link to be mutual denies a hub that power: a hub is nobody's nearest neighbour.

Judging

Cluster counts cannot rank two clusterings — an arm can look better by splitting more — so four measurements are used, and each needs the others to be readable.

Agreement over commit pairs. Given two commits, do both arms put them in the same capability? Precision and recall, with neither arm treated as truth.

A chance floor. Two clusterings share some pairs by accident, and how many depends on their shapes. Keeping each arm's exact group sizes and shuffling which commits fill them gives the floor: 0.004 to 0.012 for these shapes. Every number below is above it, and saying two arms "disagree" without this would have been saying nothing.

A stability ceiling. The model arm is not deterministic, so its agreement with anything cannot exceed its agreement with itself. Three runs over identical input give that ceiling.

Naming, from three views. A group nobody can name is a bad group whichever method produced it — the test the epoch work applies to periods. One prompt names every multi-commit group, is never told which arm produced it, and may refuse. There is no neutral view: showing the readings favours the arms that group on readings, showing the paths favours the arms that group on paths, and showing only the commit message favours neither but is itself weak evidence. So all three are reported and their agreement is the finding.

The evidence decides the grouping; the method barely matters

reading is a deterministic clustering and model is a language model, working on the same capability names by completely different mechanisms. Against the path-based baseline they land at 0.104 and 0.112 — indistinguishable. The original two-arm result of 0.090 was not a fact about consolidation. It was a fact about what capability names are.

A sweep of 24 shaping settings — four neighbour counts by three floors, with and without commit bodies — keeps baseline vs reading below 0.15 in 22 of them, the whole range being 0.013 to 0.399. This is not an artifact of one choice of neighbour count or floor.

The model arm is more stable than cluster counts suggested

This corrects an earlier record. Two runs producing 106 and 85 capabilities were written down here as evidence that the arm "is not reproducible". Measured over pairs, three runs agree at F1 0.717 to 0.773 — substantial. The cluster count is the unstable thing; the membership is not, and counting the wrong unit exaggerated the instability.

The ceiling matters more than the correction. The model agrees with itself at ~0.74 and with every other arm at 0.11 to 0.15. Its disagreement with them is real rather than run noise, and roughly an order of magnitude larger than its disagreement with itself.

45% of commit pairs grouped by any run were grouped by all three. A pairing proposed by one run is closer to a coin flip than to a finding; a pairing all three runs make is worth something. That is the confidence measure the interpretation findings asked for, and it costs one extra consolidation run to obtain.

The judge has its own noise, and it is not small

Judging is cheap where grouping is expensive, so the same four arms were judged twice. The two passes differ by up to 7 points on the message view and by about 3 on the other two — weak evidence makes an indecisive judge. Every comparison below is read against that.

No arm groups better under the neutral view

Under the message view the arms span 48% to 53% on the first pass and 46% to 56% on the second, and they do not keep their order between passes. That spread is entirely inside the judge's noise. Four methods, two of which cost money, none distinguishable from the free one.

Interpretation explains groups it did not form

Showing the readings lifts every arm, the baseline from 50% to 70% — on groups built without ever seeing a reading, a rise far outside the judge's noise. The readings did not form those groups and cannot take credit for them. They explain them: shown a reading, the judge names a group it had just refused.

That is the separable-stages result. Group with the free clustering, describe with the readings, and pay for only one of the two.

But path grouping falls apart as groups get larger, and interpretation does not

The separable-stages conclusion holds only for small groups. reading beats baseline under the paths view — the baseline's own channel, where self-favouring runs the wrong way — 75% against 57%, and 72% against 59% on the second pass. Conditioning on group size shows why, and it is not the group-size confound it looks like:

                    2-3 commits   4-6 commits   7+ commits
  baseline              70%           54%           36%
  reading               72%           81%           60%
  model                 61%           82%           72%

The baseline is fine at pairs and triples and collapses beyond them, from 70% to 36%. The interpretation arms hold, and model improves. Paths group small clusters of adjacent work well and cannot sustain a capability; a capability that spans fourteen months is exactly what the atlas is for.

The cells are small — 14, 10 and 32 groups in the 7+ column — so this is a direction, not a coefficient. It is the strongest argument in this document that interpretation contributes something to grouping and not only to description, and it is the thing a gold set should be built to settle first.

Commit bodies: a mixed result

72.4% of the pilot's first-parent chain carries a commit body, and 445 of those 535 are merges whose subject is provenance and whose body is the pull request title. Adding them tightened the baseline slightly, 256 groups to 245. It made combined worse: its largest group went from 20 to 45 and became the project's first six months, held together by bodies reading New frontend, New data, push. And baseline vs reading did not move at all, staying at 0.104. The gap between grouping on paths and grouping on interpretation is not one the message text closes.

Two caveats, one corrected

The no-dominant-cluster rule was wrong. Across the sweep the largest baseline group ranges from 10 units to 226, and k=5 and k=8 produce groups up to 493 of 605. Those were dismissed as blobs without an external reason. The report now flags a large group as a possible umbrella that a flat arm cannot represent, rather than declaring it invalid.

Nameability is not correctness. A judge can name a group that is wrong; the three views disagree by up to 43 points on the same groups; and two passes of the same view disagree by up to 7. Nameability discriminates between methods only where they differ by more than that. Under the neutral view they do not differ at all, and in the 7+ column they do.

What follows

Do not turn the owner's list into the system's taxonomy. Giving a model those categories, sorting the pilot into them, and tuning against the result would make the solution imitate one repository owner's memory. Keep the list held out and let it audit what autonomous discovery missed.

  1. Add proposal-to-map novelty review and one bounded follow-up evidence round to the staged hierarchy critic.
  2. Repeat that unchanged staged condition and measure proposal/evidence recurrence before reconciliation.
  3. Repeat the blind hierarchy and assignment experiment on a structurally different repository.
  4. Put per-commit readings and candidate hierarchies in the viewer with their interpretation status and cited Git evidence visible.

The assignment-disagreement critic now has three candidate-visible and three blind samples. Exact decisions agree by 85.9% within the visible arm, 71.6% within the blind arm, and 54.5% across arms. That separation confirms candidate-ID anchoring on this fixed experiment and keeps the blind critic as the default source of hierarchy-review signals.

The first hierarchy critic now consumes those signals without receiving an owner taxonomy or mutating the map. From 83 supplied structural signals it proposed two candidate changes: add dependency-update automation beneath operations, and test splitting the growth/analytics umbrella into its existing children. Both are evidence-cited and still unapplied. A single run demonstrates the decision interface, not proposal correctness or stability.

A focused-packet experiment then retained all recurring gaps and unmatched alternatives plus the 24 lowest-overlap boundary comparisons. Changing context changed the capable model's secondary proposals, confirming that retrieval policy is part of the semantic method. Three cheap-model repeats had only 24.9% mean operation-target F1 and failed signal coverage, even though dependency automation recurred 3/3 with identical evidence. The system now records that distinction: recurring proposals from an incomplete condition are not eligible to revise the map.

The first staged-retrieval run then used a cheap model to plan every signal, a deterministic broker to reduce 69 requested readings to a 32-reading budget, and a capable model for the decision. Every signal retained evidence and the final review was complete. Peak input fell 34% and cost fell 51%, while total two-stage input rose 25%. Dependency automation recurred; three new additions did not appear in the static run, and two overlap existing node descriptions. Staging is now operational, but it needs an explicit novelty/boundary gate before it can replace full-context review.
